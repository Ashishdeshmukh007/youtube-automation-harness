# Brand Bible — Chariot of the Self

> The single source of truth for the channel's identity. The scriptwriter,
> researcher, visual-designer, voice-engineer, and publisher all read this.
> When in doubt, this file wins.

## The name

**Chariot of the Self.** From the Katha Upanishad:

> "Know the Self as the rider in the chariot, the body as the chariot, the
> intellect as the charioteer, the mind as the reins."

The Self is the still passenger. The mind is not the master — it is the reins.
Every video is, in some sense, about taking the reins.

## One-line promise

**Ancient Indian wisdom (Gita, Upanishads, Yoga Sutras, Chanakya) for the modern
mind — anxiety, overthinking, burnout, inner peace.**

The promise has two halves and the order matters:

1. **Lead with the pain, not the scripture.** A viewer searching "how to stop
   overthinking" needs to see that pain in our title and thumbnail before any
   Sanskrit term. The teaching earns the click; the title earns the impression.
2. **End with the practice.** Every video finishes with one specific thing the
   viewer can do *this week* — when, where, how long. If they can't do it
   Tuesday, rewrite it.

## What the channel is

Each video takes **one modern problem** — burnout, anxiety, anger, identity,
fear, comparison, restlessness, grief — and shows **what ancient India already
knew about it**, drawing on:

- **Bhagavad Gita** — action without attachment, duty, steadiness of mind
- **Upanishads** — the Self, the witness, the nature of the mind
- **Yoga Sutras (Patanjali)** — the mechanics of attention, the kleshas, practice
- **Chanakya (Arthashastra / Niti)** — strategy, discipline, dealing with people

Every video **ends with a concrete practice the viewer can use this week.**

## What the channel is NOT

- **Practice over preaching.** We don't demand belief or worship; every video ends
  in something the viewer can actually do — *"the oldest operating manual for the
  human mind, still running."* We honor the tradition and depict its figures
  reverently in their classic, recognizable forms.
- **Not a lecture.** Not academic. No Sanskrit-heavy jargon without plain meaning.
- **Not self-help fluff.** No empty hustle-positivity. Earn every claim from a text
  or a clear line of reasoning.
- **Not guru-coded.** We are a fellow traveller pointing at an old map, not a master.

## Voice & tone

- Calm, grounded, unhurried. Speaks to one person, late at night.
- Plain modern English. Short sentences. Concrete images over abstraction.
- Warm but not saccharine. Serious but not heavy. A little awe is allowed.
- Sanskrit terms: introduce, translate once in plain words, then mostly use the
  English. (e.g. *vairagya* — "non-attachment, the loosening of the grip.")
- Always attribute: name the text/figure when you state what "ancient India knew."

## Per-video structure (the spine)

The arc below is **time-coded** — pacing is part of the structure, not a vibe.
These are the chunks YouTube's retention graph cares about.

1. **0:00–0:15 — The hook.** Name the modern problem *and* make a surprising
   claim. Two sentences, no Sanskrit yet. Example: *"You're not anxious because
   life is uncertain. You're anxious because you think you are this small
   person in the story. The Upanishads completely disagree."* If we lose them
   here, the rest doesn't matter — so the very first `Shot:` must match the
   hook visually (the viewer sees their pain, not generic mountains).
2. **0:15–1:00 — The turn.** A relatable modern scenario (office, phone at
   night, Sunday dread) followed by a clear promise: *"By the end of this,
   you'll have a 2-minute practice you can use this week."* Name the text or
   figure here — Katha Upanishad, Bhagavad Gita, Patanjali — and translate any
   Sanskrit term once in plain English.
3. **1:00–7:00 — The teaching.** Unpack the idea in plain terms, with a story
   or image from the text. One central idea per video, not five. Two or three
   clear beats, each with its own `Shot:` / `Clip:` moment. Use a `Clip:` beat
   only for the most dramatic moments (Krishna, Arjuna, the chariot, the
   turning point) — see visual-prompting skill.
4. **7:00–7:30 — The bridge.** Connect the teaching precisely back to the
   modern problem the viewer named in the hook. Make it concrete: *what does
   this look like in their Tuesday?*
5. **7:30–8:00 — The practice (the payoff).** One specific, doable thing for
   *this week*: when, where, how long. *"Tomorrow morning, before you touch
   your phone, sit 90 seconds and watch your breath."* Not "meditate more" —
   "meditate *this*, *here*, *for this long*." If they can't do it Tuesday,
   rewrite it.
6. **8:00–8:15 — The close + bridge.** A single resonant line (often circles
   back to the chariot), then a soft pointer to a related next video —
   *"If this helped you see your mind differently, watch 'Why You Can't Stop
   Overthinking' next."* The bridge sells the next video and keeps them on the
   channel.

Length: **8–15 minutes total** (~1,300–2,200 spoken words). The pacing above
scales — at 15 min, the "teaching" beat stretches to 12 min and the others
hold their timing.

## Visual identity

- Slow, cinematic, contemplative. Visuals breathe; nothing is fast or busy.
- Palette: rich, warm cinematic color with deep contrast and gold highlights,
  dawn/dusk light. Vivid traditional colors (Krishna-blue, saffron, gold) are
  welcome on the figures.
- Motifs: Himalayan foothills and rivers, weathered palm-leaf manuscripts, a lone
  chariot or horse at distance, oil lamps, still water, hands, old stone, banyan
  trees, the night sky.
- **Cinematic and reverent — depict the figures traditionally.** Krishna and Arjuna
  (and other deities/epic figures) appear in their classic, recognizable forms —
  Krishna blue-skinned with mukut, peacock feather and flute; Arjuna the crowned
  warrior-prince with his bow — rendered as living, cinematic film characters, not
  flat calendar-poster or AI-cartoon art. No anime, no neon "om", no modern/Western
  clothing.
- **Everyone depicted is Indian / South Asian.** Main figures (Krishna, Arjuna,
  Chanakya, …) and any incidental people are Indian. Western characters are added
  only when a specific topic genuinely calls for one.
- No on-screen text overlays during the video (captions ship as a separate YouTube
  subtitle track). The frame stays clean.

## Music

Restrained ambient under the voice. Slow drone, subtle bansuri/tanpura, no
percussion, no melody that competes with the words. Modern and understated, not
devotional. Mixed low (music sits far under the voiceover).

## Thumbnail & titles

Thumbnails and titles are a unit. They earn the click *together* — neither alone.

**Thumbnail — emotional visual + 2–4 words.**

- One clear **emotional visual**: a stressed face, a calm face at sunrise, a hand
  on a chest, a chariot wheel, a still mind reflected in water. Something the
  viewer *feels*, not just sees.
- **2–4 bold words** in large type. The modern pain or the immediate promise.
  Examples: "STOP OVERTHINKING", "INNER PEACE", "END ANXIETY", "LET GO".
- **Avoid scripture art** (Krishna portraits, temple imagery, Sanskrit calligraphy
  as the headline). Those thumbnails look devotional and undersell the practical
  promise. Save the figures for the video body, where they earn their place.
- High contrast, single focal point, readable at thumbnail size.

**Titles — pain first, scripture second.**

- Lead with the modern problem. Hint at the source in the second half.
- Templates that work in this niche:
  - "Stop [X] – [Y]'s most ignored lesson" (e.g., "Stop Overthinking – Bhagavad
    Gita's Most Ignored Lesson")
  - "The [time period] cure for [modern pain] ([source])" (e.g., "The 2,000-year-old
    cure for burnout (Bhagavad Gita)")
  - "Why you [symptom] — and what [source] says" (e.g., "Why you can't stop
    scrolling — and what the Yoga Sutras say")
- Avoid: pure Sanskrit titles ("Dhyana in the Bhagavad Gita"), vague promises
  ("The Truth About Peace"), clickbait that disrespects the source.

**Series scaffolds** (use the same thumbnail/title shape across an arc):

- "What the Gita knew about [topic]" — work, anger, duty, results, fear, action
- "The mind, according to Patanjali" — attention, distraction, habit
- "Chanakya on [topic]" — enemies, money, discipline, friends
- "The Self and the witness" — identity, the observer, ego

A viewer who watches one video in a series should *want* the next — the title and
thumbnail of #2 should promise a related teaching, not a topic pivot.

## Recurring series (idea bank)

- "What the Gita knew about ____" (work, anger, duty, results)
- "The mind, according to Patanjali" (attention, distraction, habit)
- "Chanakya on ____" (enemies, money, discipline, friends)
- "The Self and the witness" (identity, the observer, ego)

## Hard lines (never cross)

- Never misquote or invent a verse. If unsure of attribution, say so or cut it.
- Never claim ancient texts "predicted" modern science. Draw honest parallels only.
- Never frame another tradition as wrong. We illuminate, we don't attack.
- Never let the practice be vague. If the viewer can't do it Tuesday, rewrite it.
